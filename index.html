Your eye judges a colour relative to whatever surrounds it. The same gray looks dark on white paper and light on black — it hasn't changed, your perception has.

Neutral mid-gray is the standard because it biases you least. It's why galleries use gray walls and why photographers carry gray cards.

Use white or black deliberately: if your painting will sit on white paper, judge it on white. Otherwise stay on gray.

Palette

Most palette tools return the colours that cover the most area of a photo. For a painter that's the wrong question — it hands you five variations of mud and skips the one bright note that made you pick the reference.

Tune for painting forces three things in: the darkest dark, the lightest light, and the strongest accent. Those are what a painting is built on.

Turn it off to see the difference. If the tuned version doesn't look more useful to you, trust your eye over the tool.

Colours

Tap a swatch or its code to copy the hex.

Tap the photo to add any colour the extraction missed — a reflected light, a shadow edge, the one warm spot in a cold picture.

Name colours as switches the labels between hex codes and the nearest colour in a supply set — coloured pencils, or Winsor & Newton Winton oils.

These values are approximations of how each pencil or tube appears in use — close enough to say "start with these two", not a colour-managed match. Laydown shifts with pressure, paper and layering; paint shifts even more, since masstone straight from the tube, a thin glaze and a tint with white are three different colours from one tube.

Paint especially is a mixing medium. A 41-colour range cannot contain the thousands of colours in a photograph, so treat a named tube as the closest place to start mixing from, not the answer.

When the nearest pencil isn't actually close, the row says rough match. Believe it — that means your set has nothing near that colour and you'll need to layer.

Value scale

Value is how light or dark a colour is, ignoring what colour it is. A saturated red and a dull green can share a value — squint and they merge.

Value does most of the work in a picture. You can get every hue wrong and still have a painting that reads, but get the values wrong and nothing saves it.

Watch where the dots land. Bunched in the middle is the classic beginner signature — a timid painting with no real darks and no real lights. Good references usually spread.

Mixer

Paint does not mix like light. Two lights add together; two paints each subtract, and the mixture reflects only what both let through. That's why averaging colour codes gives the wrong answer — it predicts blue and yellow make grey, when any painter knows they make green.

This uses Kubelka–Munk mixing, the standard model for opaque paint films: each colour is converted to an absorption-over-scattering ratio, those are mixed in proportion, and the result converted back.

Still test a swatch. Proper Kubelka–Munk wants measured spectral data for each pigment. Working from three channels of an approximate tube colour is a coarse model, and it can't know your medium, your ground, or how thickly you paint. Treat a recipe as a reasoned starting point, not a formula.

Untick what you don't own. A recipe calling for a tube you don't have is useless. The search only uses ticked colours.

The tray is for the part after the recipe. Press use on any suggestion to load it, then add a touch more white, try a third pigment, or build a colour the search never reached. The two halves of the well meet with no seam because colours are judged at their shared edge — a gap between them makes a near match look worse than it is.

ΔE is how far the mix lands from the target: under 3 is indistinguishable, over 22 means your set genuinely cannot reach that colour and no mixing will get you there.

Framing

A reference is rarely framed the way you want to paint it. Cropping changes which colours dominate, so it changes the palette too — crop first, then pull your colours.

Straighten before gridding. A tilted horizon makes nonsense of a grid, because every cell inherits the tilt.

Rotating shows thirds guides — line the horizon up against one. The image scales up slightly as it turns so no empty corners appear.

Both rewrite the working image, so every other tool sees the new framing. The original is kept and Undo framing restores it.

Adjust

For fixing a bad photograph — an underexposed reference, a flat grey phone snap, a scan that lost its colour.

These all feed the palette. Unlike the study tools, correcting the image changes the colours that get extracted, because you're deciding this is what the subject really looked like.

Auto-fix reads the photo and moves these sliders. It measures — midpoint, tonal range, colour cast, noise, clipping — and sets each control accordingly. Nothing is hidden and nothing is invented: it only adjusts what you could have adjusted yourself, and you can override any of it.

Start with white balance. It's the error you can't see — your eye corrected for the room's light while you were standing in it, but the camera didn't. Every colour in a warm-lit photo is warm, and no other slider fixes that.

Vibrancy is not saturation. Saturation scales every colour equally and blows out the ones already strong. Vibrancy pushes the dull colours hardest, so skin and skies survive it.

Noise reduction earns its place here. Sensor speckle in the shadows creates clusters that were never in the scene, and drags the darkest-dark pick onto a stray noise pixel. Cleaning it up makes the palette more honest, not just the picture prettier.

Highlight recovery has a hard limit. Where all three channels are pure white the data is simply gone — nothing can bring it back, and anything that claims to is inventing. Where only one or two channels clipped, the survivors still carry the hue and can be pulled back honestly. Turn on Show clipped areas to see which you're dealing with.

Go gently. A photo pushed hard gives a palette that looks exciting on screen and garish on canvas.

Study tools

off

Flattens the photo into a few flat values — the exercise usually called a notan or value study.

Try three steps. Everything becomes light, mid, or dark, and the big shapes of the picture appear. Those shapes are the composition; the detail is decoration on top.

Beginners paint what they know is there rather than what's there. This makes that impossible to keep doing.

off

Every painting teacher says "squint at it" — narrowing your eyes throws away detail and leaves the big masses and value relationships.

Do it to your reference and to your own work. If the two blur into a similar arrangement of light and dark, you're on track, however rough the detail.

The low steps (soft) are the ones you'll use most — just enough to drop detail while shapes stay readable. Push to hard or masses when you want the picture reduced to a few big blocks of value, which is how you check composition.

off

Lifts edge detail in a soft photograph, for when you're trying to read exactly where a form turns.

This is a way of looking, not a correction — so it never touches the palette. Sharpening works by exaggerating the difference across every edge, which invents a bright and a dark pixel on either side of it. Those halos are colours that were never in the scene, and letting them into the extraction would put them in your palette.

The opposite of Squint. Use it to check detail, then squint again to check the whole reads.

off

Collapses everything into two shapes: subject and not-subject. It's the oldest test in drawing — if the silhouette doesn't read, the drawing doesn't read, however good the interior detail is.

Use it on a pose. If you can't tell what the figure is doing from the black shape alone, the pose needs changing before you render anything.

Drag the cut point around. There's no correct setting — moving it decides what counts as the shape, and finding where the subject separates cleanly from its background is itself the exercise.

Turn Squint up first. Blurring before you threshold gives a cleaner, less speckled shape — the same reason painters squint before blocking in.

After twenty minutes on a drawing your eye stops seeing it and starts seeing what you meant. Flipping breaks that — errors in tilt, proportion and symmetry jump straight out.

This only works if you flip your own work too. Hold it to a mirror, or photograph it and flip the photo. Comparing a mirrored reference to an unmirrored drawing tells you nothing.

Check every twenty minutes or so, not at the end.

Grid

Divide the reference into cells, draw the same cells on your paper, then copy one cell at a time. It turns an overwhelming whole into a series of small, achievable shapes.

Fewer cells is harder and teaches more. Start at 3×3 or 4×4. A 16×16 grid will get you an accurate drawing while teaching you almost nothing.

Wean off it. The grid is a scaffold, not a method — an artist who always grids never learns to measure by eye. Use it for a few pieces, then try the same subject without it.

Square cells keeps proportions honest. Rule of thirds is a different tool: it's for composition, not copying.

Calibrate

Every supply set here carries approximate values — how a pencil or tube looks in use, judged by eye rather than measured.

Even measured data would be wrong for you. The same pencil lays down differently on hot-press than on toothy paper, at different pressures, under different light. The only value truly right for your work is the one off your own swatch.

To correct a colour: swatch your set on the paper you actually use, photograph it in even daylight, load that photo as the reference, then press set beside a colour and tap its swatch in the photo.

Corrections apply everywhere at once — naming, the mixer and the printouts. They're saved on this device; export them to move them or keep a backup.

Shoot the photo carefully. Flat even light, no shadow across the chart, no auto white-balance shifting between shots. A photo taken under warm indoor light will calibrate every colour warm.
